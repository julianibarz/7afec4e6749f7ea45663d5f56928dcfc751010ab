01

How the resistive backup behaves

Every integrated hybrid has a heat pump plus one or two ~4.5 kW resistance elements in the tank. The elements are recovery accelerators, not on-demand heaters: the tank stays stratified (hot at the top, where the outlet draws), so a shower stays at full temperature until the tank is nearly spent. Sensors watch the upper-tank temperature; when a heavy draw pulls it below a threshold, the upper element fires within seconds and reheats the top ~20 gallons in about 40 minutes while the heat pump works underneath.

The practical rule: tank size protects the shower you're in; the backup element shortens the wait before the next one. Which is why sizing the tank right matters more than backup wattage.

Heat pump only

~1 kW · Max efficiency, elements never fire. Recovery ~8–12 gal/hr. Fine for schedule-heated overnight + midday solar runs.

Hybrid / Auto

1–5.5 kW · Factory default. Heat pump primary; upper element assists automatically after tub fills or back-to-back showers.

High demand / Turbo

~5.5 kW · Elements + compressor together. House-guests mode; recovery ≈ standard electric tank.

Electric only

4.5 kW · Compressor off — a service fallback, not an operating mode.

03

Are tanks and heat pumps interchangeable?

Depends entirely on the architecture — this is the real fork in the decision:

A · Integrated one-piece

Rheem · A.O. Smith · Bradford White · LG

Compressor is factory-sealed on top of the tank — one appliance. If the tank fails, the whole unit is replaced.

Tank swap? No. But the hookup (standard plumbing + 30 A/240 V circuit) is universal, so the whole appliance swaps across brands in an afternoon. You re-choose brand every ~10–15 yr.

B · Split, matched pair

SANCO2 (CO₂ split system)

Outdoor CO₂ compressor + indoor stainless tank, connected by plain water lines. Tank is silent; compressor lives outside.

Tank swap? Within the ecosystem. Tank and heat pump replace independently, and the 43/83/119-gal tanks interchange — but only SANCO2-to-SANCO2. No cross-brand mixing (matched controls & sensors).

C · Custom air-to-water

Chiltrix, Arctic, etc.

A generic air-to-water heat pump feeding any indirect tank; can also serve radiant floors.

Tank swap? Fully mix-and-match — but installer-dependent, niche support, and overkill unless you're doing hydronic space heating too. Not recommended here.

08

Stress tests: the 45-minute shower

A 45-minute shower at a standard 2.5 gpm head is 112 gallons of mixed water — 63 gallons of 140 °F hot water per shower. That's more than most families use in a day, so long showers, not tub fills, are actually the sizing driver for this house. The four-shower event pulls ~47 kW electrical (two boosters + two elements + compressors) — comfortable on 320 A service (~77 kW), far beyond two Powerwalls (23 kW), so the boosters should be on load-shed relays that lock them out during outages. That config — two 80-gal hybrids + two 18 kW boosters, one set per floor — runs ≈ $11,500–12,000 installed, ~$450/yr, ≈ $16k over 10 years. The pooled variant (§09: tanks in parallel on a common trunk, boosters in series on it) uses the same hardware but passes every row with margin — 45 kW continuous + 24 kWh stored against a worst case of 66 kW — and is immune to which rooms the load lands in, where the per-floor split fails if three showers happen to share a floor.

Without boosters, the three-simultaneous case breaks everything — including tankless: a 36 kW electric tankless at 7.5 gpm can only lift 60 °F water to ~93 °F (needs ~50 kW for shower temp), and even a max-size gas tankless barely holds 105 °F. It's a storage problem, not a heater problem. Three practical fixes, in order of sanity: (1) 1.8 gpm shower heads — cuts every demand 28 %, turning the two-simultaneous case into a pass for the SANCO2 119 and nearly for per-floor 80s; (2) stagger starts by ~45 min, letting the element recover between; (3) brute force — two SANCO2 119-gal systems (~230 gal of 145 °F storage) covers even this one.

The resistive element's real contribution: during any single long shower it adds ~12–16 gallons, which is exactly the margin that turns the solo 45-minute shower from "marginal" into "covers." It cannot rescue simultaneous showers — 4.5 kW against a 2.8 gpm hot draw (~40 kW thermal) is a rounding error. Tank gallons are the only thing that survives overlap.

09

Adding backup wattage — and the solar-surplus question

More elements inside the tank?

No — fixed at the factory

Integrated units ship with two element ports (typically interlocked — one energized at a time, which is what keeps them on a 30 A circuit) and are UL-listed as a sealed appliance. There's no adding a third element, and swapping in higher-wattage ones voids the listing for a marginal gain.

The lever isn't inside the tank. It's downstream, or in storage size.

The real upgrade path

Series tankless booster

An 18 kW electric tankless plumbed after the tank, set to ~110 °F. While the tank is hot it modulates to zero and does nothing. When the tank runs lukewarm, it makes up the difference — an 18 kW unit can lift even stone-cold 60 °F water to shower temp at 2.5 gpm.

This is the original "backup that fires only when the tank is empty" idea, made electric. One 75 A circuit (trivial on 320 A service) instead of 150 A for whole-house tankless. Guarantees one endless shower; overlapping showers are still a storage problem. Optionally interlocked to shed during outages so it never touches the Powerwalls.

Sharing boosters between tanks

Yes — pool everything instead

Boosters can't be shared electrically, but they can hydraulically: plumb both tanks in parallel on one common trunk (reverse-return so they drain evenly), then put both boosters in series on that trunk. Now every fixture in the house draws on 160 gal of storage and 36 kW of boost — wherever the load lands.

The math: supply becomes 36 kW boost + 9 kW elements = 45 kW continuous with ~24 kWh stored. Three simultaneous 45-min showers: deficit 4.5 kW → 5+ hours. Four: deficit 21 kW → ~69 min. Passes everything, immune to which rooms the load hits. Trade-offs: one plumbing spine (add isolation valves so one tank can be serviced solo), and the recirc loop does the work of keeping far bathrooms instant.

"1 kW barely dents the solar surplus"

Correct — by design

At COP ~4, even a daily 45-min shower is only ~3–4 kWh of electricity. Water heating stops being an energy problem; the ~10 kW of midday surplus a big array throws off belongs to the EVs (11–19 kW), the Powerwalls, and pre-cooling the house — not the water heater.

Want water to soak more surplus? Schedule high-demand mode midday (~5.5 kW), or store bigger and hotter — a SANCO2 119-gal at 145 °F holds ~18–20 kWh thermal. But the marginal kWh is better spent in a car battery.

— cold water — hot water ▢ booster stage ┄ recirculation return

Topology A · Per-floor silos — two sealed systems

Two sealed silos: each floor gets exactly 80 gal + 18 kW, no more. If three heavy draws land on one floor, that floor is on its own while the other system sits idle — capacity exists but can't cross the wall.

Topology B · Pooled trunk — parallel tanks, series boosters

One pool: every fixture in the house draws on 160 gal of storage and 36 kW of boost (45 kW with the tank elements), wherever the load lands. Three showers on one floor is indistinguishable from three showers spread across the house — the trunk doesn't care. The dashed recirculation return keeps far bathrooms instant despite the longer runs.

Booster effect on the §08 stress tests: every single-shower row becomes unconditionally ✓, "shower then tub" passes, and — because the tank only has to cover the deficit between demand and the booster's 18 kW — even two simultaneous 45-min showers pass on a single 80-gal tank (deficit ~10.5 kW; tank lasts ~69 min). See the booster rows added to §08 for the three- and four-shower cases.

11

Panel & service sizing — where the real bottleneck is

"320 A" residential service is 400 A-class hardware (a 320 A-continuous meter base), and it almost never feeds one panel — the standard Bay Area new-build config is two 200 A panels, because 400 A-busbar residential panels are rare and expensive. That busbar, not PG&E, is where the limits live.

Amps ↔ kW decoder (everything here is 240 V split-phase, so kW = A × 240 ÷ 1,000). The planned PG&E upgrade is neither "250 A" nor "350 A" — it's a 320 A continuous service on 400 A-class hardware:

What the service supplies

NEC demand-factor math says this house calcs comfortably into a 320 A service; load management makes it true in practice.

Can Powerwalls add to it?

On-grid: yes, ~23 kW more

Grid-parallel, two Powerwall 3s discharge into the bus alongside the service — ~96 A on top of 320 A, ~100 kW total available. The constraint is code, not physics: NEC 705.12's 120 % busbar rule caps how much inverter output can land on a panel, which is why solar + storage get their own 200 A panel or a Backup Gateway (200 A rated).

Off-grid: they ARE the panel. 23 kW total (11.5 kW each; more units add power, DC expansion packs add only energy). Hence the booster shed relays.

The spec to give the electrician

Two 200 A panels + load management

Panel A behind the Backup Gateway: HPWHs, HVAC, kitchen, essentials — and the EV chargers, on dynamic load management, so cars can still sip solar surplus during an outage instead of being cut off. Panel B non-backup: only the 18 kW boosters — dumb resistance loads that deserve a hard shed.

Or replace the hard split with software — see §12 on SPAN and vehicle-to-home.

Bottleneck order: ① panel busbar (200 A each — solved by using two), ② service conductors at 320 A continuous (solved on-grid by Powerwall discharge + load management), ③ PG&E's transformer (their problem; flag total connected load on the service application so they size it right the first time). The water-heating design never stresses any of these — it's the EV chargers and boosters that need the coordination.

12

SPAN, and cars as the backup battery

SPAN smart panels

Priorities instead of panels

Two SPAN panels (200 A each) put every circuit behind backup and shed by software priority when off-grid — EVs charge whenever there's headroom (midday solar surplus) instead of being severed by a panel split. Per-circuit metering and control covers the boosters too, replacing relay logic.

Cost, priced July 2026: SPAN hardware $2,550–4,100/panel (MAIN 32 = $3,500), $5,500–8,000 installed each → two panels ≈ $13–16k, an effective premium of ≈ $8–11k over the standard panels this build buys anyway (25C credit covers up to $600). One real caveat: panels are 30-year infrastructure and SPAN is a startup — weigh vendor longevity against the flexibility. Named fallback for the bid: Lumin — a ~$3.5–5.5k retrofit controller beside ordinary panels, managing the 12 circuits that matter (boosters, pool, hot tub, EVs, HPWH DR) with zero panel lock-in; ~90 % of the §18 behavior at half the premium if SPAN's quote or lead time disappoints.

The Tesla-native alternative

Most of it, for free

Powerwall + Universal Wall Connector already coordinate: charge-on-solar, dynamic sharing between chargers, and off-grid EV charging with a battery state-of-charge floor so the cars never drain the house. That's ~80 % of what you'd use SPAN for, at no extra cost — SPAN's edge is per-circuit control of everything else and a much better picture of the house.

Vehicle-to-home (V2H)

Yes — and pre-wire for it now

Tesla Powershare does this today: 11.5 kW from the vehicle through a Universal Wall Connector + compatible Gateway — currently Cybertruck-only (~123 kWh ≈ 9 Powerwalls of stored energy), expected to spread across the lineup. With one V2H car home, an outage becomes 34.5 kW + days of energy — even the shower boosters could stay on.

New-construction spec: Powershare-compatible Gateway, Universal Wall Connectors, and oversized conduit to the garage. Costs almost nothing now; makes V2H a plug-in upgrade later.

How it lands on the water-heating decision: none of this changes the §10 ranking — but SPAN or Tesla-native load management is what lets the "never cold" config (two hybrids + boosters) coexist peacefully with two EVs on a 320 A service, and a V2H future makes the boosters outage-proof too.

13

SPAN vs no SPAN — the wiring, drawn

Both tabs are the same physical stack — grid, meter, solar, Powerwalls, two 200 A panels, same loads. What moves is where decisions live: in the copper (which panel a load is wired to) or in software (what matters right now).

State 1 · Grid up — contactor closed, the whole house is one bus

Everything is one connected network: current ignores the panel boundaries, so the Powerwalls can serve the boosters on Panel B (flowing backwards through the closed Gateway), and grid + batteries stack to ~100 kW.

The cut happens at the Gateway. Panel B was wired upstream of it, so it's stranded with the dead grid — the Powerwalls physically cannot reach it. Panel A + Powerwalls + solar carry on as a self-contained 23 kW island.

With boosters, the stack simply exceeds the service. Three layers make it degrade gracefully instead of tripping:

Layer 1 · automatic, no config

Powerwalls peak-shave

Grid-parallel, 3× Powerwall 3 add 34.5 kW on top of the 77 kW service — ≈ 111 kW total. Transients (sub bass hits, oven elements cycling, compressor starts) come from the batteries, never reaching the meter. A depleted battery on a cloudy winter night is the one gap — which is why Layer 2 exists.

Layer 2 · the shed order

Sacrifice in priority order

When measured service current crosses ~80 %, shed in order: ① EV charging (23 kW back instantly; cars resume at 2 am — nobody notices) → ② pool heat pump + pump (the water loses ~1 °F/hr; nobody notices) → ③ shower boosters (160 gal of 140 °F storage keeps showers hot; §08) → ④ hot tub heater (lid-open loss ~2 °F/hr; fine for an hour). The party — kitchen, AV, lighting, HVAC — is never shed.

Layer 3 · the mechanism

SPAN makes it config, not copper

With SPAN panels (§12–13) the entire shed order is software — per-circuit priorities, remade in real time. Tesla-native alternative: Universal Wall Connectors already do ① natively via dynamic load management; ②–④ need a CT-driven load-shed controller or contactors on three circuits — decide which before rough-in, because the boosters and pool equipment must land on controllable circuits either way.
